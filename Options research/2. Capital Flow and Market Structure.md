# Module 2 — Capital Flow and Market Structure

## Why This Module?

Our project aims to estimate institutional position changes from stock market data.

To interpret market data correctly, we first need to understand what "money flow" actually means. The term is widely used in finance, but different people often refer to completely different concepts.

This module establishes a precise vocabulary for discussing capital flow.

---

# 1. What is Money Flow?

The phrase "money flow" does not have a unique definition.

Different studies may refer to different types of flow.

Therefore, every discussion should begin by asking:

> What kind of flow are we talking about?

---

# 2. Trading Flow

Trading flow refers to transactions occurring in the secondary market.

Every completed trade always has

- one buyer
- one seller

Therefore, trading volume alone does not imply that new capital has entered the market.

Large trading volume only indicates active trading.

---

# 3. Capital Flow

Capital flow refers to new investable capital entering or leaving an asset class.

Examples include

- ETF creations
- ETF redemptions
- Mutual fund subscriptions
- Pension contributions

This represents genuine movement of capital into or out of the market.

---

# 4. ETF Secondary Market vs Primary Market

ETF trading should be separated into two markets.

## Secondary Market

Investors trade ETF shares with each other.

ETF shares outstanding remain unchanged.

The ETF manager does not buy or sell underlying stocks.

Therefore:

Secondary market trading alone does NOT represent new capital entering the underlying stocks.

---

## Primary Market

When ETF demand becomes sufficiently large, Authorized Participants (APs) perform

- Creation
- Redemption

During creation, APs purchase the underlying basket of stocks and exchange it for newly created ETF shares.

This process creates genuine buying pressure in the underlying stocks.

Therefore:

ETF Net Creation is a much better proxy for capital inflow than ETF trading volume.

---

# 5. Position Flow

Our project is ultimately interested in changes in institutional positions rather than trading activity itself.

Institutional positions cannot be directly observed.

Instead, we observe market variables that may contain information about position changes.

Examples include

- Order Flow Imbalance (OFI)
- Large Trades (Main Flow)
- VWAP behaviour
- Price Impact

These observable variables act as proxies for the underlying institutional position.

---

# 6. Options and Stock Market

The stock market does not operate independently.

Option trading can indirectly influence stock market behaviour.

A simplified transmission mechanism is

Institution trades options

↓

Dealer adjusts option inventory

↓

Dealer hedges in the stock market

↓

Stock order flow changes

↓

Observed market features change

Therefore, some patterns observed in stock data may originate from option market activity rather than stock investors themselves.

---

# Connection to Our Project

Our current model estimates institutional position changes using stock market observations.

Current observable variables include

- Large trades
- OFI
- VWAP
- Price impact

Future research should recognise that these observables may also be affected by activity originating from the option market.

At the current stage, we do not explicitly model option market effects.

However, understanding this transmission mechanism helps us interpret model outputs more carefully.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Trading volume is not the same as capital inflow.

2. ETF secondary-market trading is different from ETF creation and redemption.

3. Institutional positions are hidden variables.

4. Market features are proxies rather than direct observations.

5. Option market activity can influence stock market behaviour through dealer hedging.
